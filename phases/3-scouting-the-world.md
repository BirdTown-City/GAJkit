# Phase 3: Scouting report on the world

**What it gives the candidate:** a clear, honest picture of the market for someone like them, so they stop guessing what employers want and can see a realistic path to their goal. They come out with an approved direction and a few next-role objectives.

**Before you start:** the candidate has an approved candidate profile and an interview-ready set (Phase 2).

## Rules for this phase

- **The candidate stays at the center.** Market evidence informs their choices; it never replaces their goal or their say.
- **Market evidence is evidence.** Postings, recruiter messages and employer statements are dated, sourced samples, not facts about the candidate.
- **Research is bounded.** Answer from what you already have first. Look at postings only to answer a named question, and set a stopping point before you start. "Not enough evidence yet" is a valid result.
- **Read the requirements properly.** A posting's requirements decide qualification; its duties describe the work. See "Reading a posting" in `phases/5-search-and-applying.md`. A new duty the candidate hasn't done before isn't a gap unless the employer actually requires prior experience of it.

## Step 1. Translate strengths into what employers buy

Turn the candidate's strengths, preferences and goal into a short list of skill groups and role families that employers actually hire for, in the words employers use. For each, name the employer problem it solves and the candidate's strongest supporting stories.

Employer vocabulary is search language, not a new claim; claim boundaries stay as approved. Keep strengths the market clearly buys even if they're off the goal; the candidate decides whether to use them.

## Step 2. Look at current and recent openings

For each role family that matters to the goal, look at a small, explicitly bounded sample of current and recent openings, including counterexamples. Before you start, write down:

- the question you're answering;
- where you'll look and how you'll choose;
- the practical scope, such as location and level;
- when you'll stop.

Stop when the question is answered or the bound is reached. Keep "I didn't search widely enough" separate from "there's little demand". Don't contact anyone while researching.

## Step 3. Compare the market with the candidate

For each role family, say plainly which it is:

- **LIKELY NOW**: roles they're likely to be hired for now, and why.
- **NEEDS EVIDENCE**: roles they have the skills for but would need to show more evidence for, and what evidence would help.
- **STEPPING STONE**: a role between where they are and the goal that builds on their strengths and closes their gaps.
- **NOT NOW**: roles blocked by an explicit requirement they don't meet.

Where it helps, look through six lenses: the employer's problem; who buys and how much demand there is; the reason to hire; access and requirements; whether the candidate can be found; and how the role fits their future.

For each direction you're proposing, write one hiring thesis that could be proved wrong:

> Employers facing [problem] hire [role] for [outcome]. This candidate is credible because [evidence]. The screens that matter are [requirements]. Useful search cues are [language and channels]. The assumption most worth testing is [assumption], and the smallest test that could change this is [test].

Report personal fit, market demand, eligibility and observed employer acceptance separately. A different past title isn't disqualifying; transferable ability doesn't override an explicit requirement. If you need personal facts or preferences you don't have, ask for them in one short batch.

## Step 4. Test the direction

Test the goal against the evidence, the candidate's engagement and the market. Give it one of these, as a proposal:

- **CONFIRMED**: the evidence and their engagement support it. (That doesn't prove employers will say yes.)
- **TRANSLATION**: they've already been doing much of the target work under other titles.
- **DEVELOPMENTAL**: still wanted, but real gaps need a stepping stone or a plan.
- **REFINED**: a nearby version explains the evidence and engagement better.
- **BROADENED**: several role families share the same underlying work.
- **REJECTED**: the evidence or their reflection no longer supports it.
- **UNRESOLVED**: you need a bit more of Phase 2 first. Go back only for what's needed.

Don't favor the goal just because it came first. Keep evidence of ability separate from evidence of interest. Market uncertainty alone doesn't make a direction unresolved, and doesn't stop exploration the candidate wants.

## Step 5. Update the gap plan against the market

Take each important gap from the gap plan and check it against what employers actually screen for. For each, record in `candidate-profile.md`:

- whether it matters to the market for this direction, and the evidence;
- how it'll be handled: BUILD, SHOW EVIDENCE, WORK AROUND or BE UPFRONT;
- an honest one- or two-sentence way to talk about it in interviews; it keeps the gap, stresses credible transfer and never pretends coverage;
- the next action, if any.

## Step 6. The candidate authorizes a direction

With the candidate:

- accept, refine, defer or reject the direction;
- confirm or reject each proposed interpretation about direction;
- agree a small set of numbered next-role objectives, normally three to five, naming the stepping-stone role if one is chosen;
- note any limit they set on how far career growth may outrank pay or practicality.

Write it in "Direction and next-role objectives" in `candidate-profile.md`. Only authorized objectives steer the search.

## Step 7. Check they got there

Ask, briefly:

- Can you say what employers would most likely hire you for now, and why?
- Can you name the stepping-stone role, if there is one, and what it would build?
- For your most important gap, do you know how you'll handle it and what you'd say?

A "not yet" points to the part of the report to revisit.

**Move on** when the candidate has authorized a direction (or a clearly bounded exploratory one) and the objectives.

## Refreshing it later

Rerun only the affected steps when the regular search review, repeated employer feedback or a change in the candidate's goal shows the report is out of date. One unusual job doesn't redefine the market.
