# Phase 6: Interviews and offer

**What it gives the candidate:** they enter each conversation knowing the other side, their own plan and their best stories, with preparation and practice suited to them. They leave with lessons that make the next one better. When an offer comes, they decide on the actual work and environment, not on title or pay alone.

**Starts when:** an application reaches screening or interview, or the candidate wants to prepare for an important recruiter call, networking conversation or offer discussion.

## Rules for this phase

- **Same candidate, new setting.** Interviews use the same approved Story Bank and candidate profile as the resume. Spoken claims follow the rules in `START-HERE.md`.
- **Retrieval, not scripts.** Preparation reactivates real stories. It never creates memorized speeches or invented examples.
- **Interviewing is two-way.** The candidate is proving fit and evaluating the employer at the same time.
- **Responsibility needs authority.** High responsibility without decision rights, credible escalation, resources or sponsor support is a real fit risk.
- **The candidate makes every decision in the interview.** You don't record interviews and you give no live help during them.
- **Research on people stays public, professional and relevant.**
- **Feedback is evidence, not a command.** One interviewer's reaction or one rejection doesn't rewrite who the candidate is.

## Preparing for a conversation

Plan each conversation in proportion to its stakes. Only the first step is required. Plain conversation with the candidate is always a valid way to prepare.

**Completion contract:** scope is `CONVERSATION:<id>:PREPARATION`. `P6-C01` requires a candidate-agreed preparation scope from Step 1. `P6-C02` requires evidence that every selected part is done; if the candidate reduces the scope or defers practice, record the amended scope and remaining limit rather than reporting the original scope complete. `P6-C03` requires recorded claim boundaries, relevant logistics checks and preparation limits. All three must PASS under the shared transition contract before this preparation is COMPLETE. STANDARD/FULL includes the agreed rehearsal; typed practice, notes and other aids are valid. Apply `RS-01` through `RS-08` to external method choices and current employer/platform facts. No result here completes the whole phase or search.

The parts:

- **Scouting Report**: understand the other side, the setting and the stakes.
- **Game Plan**: the candidate's goal, their stories, a few if-then options, an optional opening, and what not to claim.
- **Call Sheet**: a one-page view to carry in, or a decision that none is needed.
- **Rehearsal**: practise in a useful format, spoken or typed with aids as needed, and check logistics.
- **Film Review**: afterwards, what was intended, what happened, and what to keep, change or leave open.
- **Self-scout**: patterns in the candidate's own conduct across several interviews.

Record the work in an interview plan, from the layout at the end of this file, saved beside the application note.

### Step 1. Set the purpose and size the effort

Write one line: what a good outcome looks like, what to avoid, and how much preparation it's worth. Pick a level:

- **LIGHT**: a quick call or early screen. The purpose, the top two or three stories, and logistics. A few minutes.
- **STANDARD**: a normal interview round. Add the Scouting Report, the Game Plan and a short rehearsal.
- **FULL**: a final round, a panel or anything high-stakes. Every part, including a Call Sheet and a fuller rehearsal.

The candidate can move up or down a level at any time.

### Step 2. Confirm the context and logistics

Stage, date, time and time zone, interviewers and their roles, format, known agenda, the link or location, materials and setup, and a fallback. Tie the preparation to this conversation, not to generic practice.

### Step 3. Scouting Report: the employer and the role

Reuse the application note's pitch where it's still current. Summarize:

- what they're buying, and the three or four central functions;
- likely concerns and hard screens;
- the candidate's hiring-credibility strengths and gaps;
- why this candidate is plausible.

Don't invent a new pitch just for variety.

### Step 4. Scouting Report: the company and the interviewers

Where current public information would help, summarize only what's useful: company priorities and recent changes, role-related news, the interviewers' public professional background and perspective, and the questions this context raises. Mark fact and inference. No trivia.

### Step 5. Game Plan

Agree with the candidate:

- the one or two things they most want the interviewers to remember;
- the top stories for this conversation, chosen from the interview-ready set in `story-bank.md`, with a backup for each where useful;
- a few if-then options, for example: if they press on a known gap, give the honest answer from the gap plan; if they ask about a skill the stories don't cover, use the nearest true example and say so;
- an opening the candidate can drop, such as a short answer to "tell me about yourself";
- what not to claim.

For each story note: the cue, what it's for, the candidate's role and authority, the key action, the supported outcome, the lesson and the claim boundary. Keep what they owned, influenced, supported and learned distinct. Choose stories by evidence fit, not novelty, and don't create a story just because a common question exists.

### Step 6. Map the likely questions to stories

Two sources of questions:

1. **Common themes**, picking those that fit: the largest or most complex program; ambiguity; failing or troubled work; influence without authority; cross-functional leadership; executive communication; prioritization and tradeoffs; deadline pressure; technical troubleshooting; leading specialists; a mistake or failure; a difficult stakeholder or challenging upward; performance management; developing people; process improvement; metrics and a single source of truth; risk and governance; learning quickly.
2. **What this conversation is likely to raise**, anticipated from the Scouting Report: the employer's problem, the role's central functions, the interviewers' perspectives and the company's current situation.

Map each to: question or topic, then primary story, then backup story, then a boundary note. It's a retrieval aid, not scripted prose.

### Step 7. Prepare the other answers

As relevant, prepare cues (not scripts) for: tell me about yourself; what are you looking for; why did you leave; why this level; how technical are you, or how do you lead engineers; leadership style; why this role and company; why should we hire you; and known gap questions, using the gap plan's honest wording.

The answers match the candidate profile and positioning. "Why this company" may use current research, not flattery. A gap answer keeps the gap and stresses credible transfer.

### Step 8. The answer shape

For behavioral questions, a useful shape is:

> the problem, then why it was hard, then what I recognized, then what I personally did, then the result or what changed, then what I learned.

It's a shape, not wording. Aim for about 60 to 90 seconds unless the interviewer clearly wants more. If interrupted, answer the follow-up rather than finishing the story.

### Step 9. Questions for the candidate to ask

Choose a small number for this stage. Don't interrogate, and don't spend scarce time on what's already known. Prefer questions that draw concrete examples.

Themes: what success looks like; decision authority against accountability; how priorities and tradeoffs are handled; how bad news and escalation are handled; manager behavior, dissent and blame; workload, nights, weekends and on-call; process maturity; why the role is open and team health; real examples of growth and promotion; work arrangement and travel; pay, when the stage is right; and anything that would confirm whether the role serves a next-role objective.

Good questions include:

- "Six months from now, what would make you say hiring me was clearly the right decision?"
- What decisions does this role make directly, and what does it escalate?
- What gets dropped when a new top priority arrives?
- What causes strong people to struggle in this role?
- What do nights, weekends and travel actually look like?

By stage:

- **Recruiter screen**: pay range, work arrangement, travel, reporting line, why the role is open, and any knockout conditions.
- **Hiring manager**: outcomes, decision authority, priorities, management style, workload, the role's history, and examples of success and failure.
- **Peers or panel**: whether the manager's description matches daily reality.
- **Executive**: strategic intent, constraints, decision rights, appetite for change, and what they expect the role to change.
- **Offer stage**: pay, bonus mechanics, equity, promotion mechanics, benefits, real time off, travel, on-call and anything still unclear.

What the posting says about scope stays "as posted" until conversations confirm it.

### Step 10. Call Sheet

For FULL, and optional for STANDARD: one page with the purpose, context, the employer's problem, the career cue, what they should remember, the if-then options, the top stories, the question map, the other answers, gap reminders, the questions to ask, open practical questions and the company context. Compact enough to study; never a script bank, and never with internal-only details. Or record that none is needed.

### Step 11. Rehearse and check logistics

For STANDARD and FULL, or whenever the candidate asks:

- the candidate practises the opening and top stories conversationally, spoken or typed with aids as useful, approximating the actual format where practical;
- you ask the likely follow-ups and one hard question, and give brief, specific feedback;
- check the link or address, time zone, start time, materials, setup and a fallback.

Practise telling, never memorizing; vary the wording between run-throughs where useful. The goal is usable preparation, not a performance threshold. Record the format actually practised and any relevant format or logistics limit. If practice is deferred, record that choice and limit rather than marking rehearsal done.

### Step 12. Film Review, afterwards

Soon after the conversation, capture: what was intended; what was actually asked; what landed well and poorly; new facts about the role or environment; stage or outcome news; feedback received; the candidate's own reaction to the fit; and what to **KEEP**, **CHANGE** next time and leave **OPEN**.

Then route it:

- a story that should be told differently, or a new story: into the journal, then "Keeping the Story Bank current" in Phase 2, then the interview-ready set;
- the application's stage: Applications in `tracker.md`;
- market or search learning: the journal, for the regular review in Phase 5;
- a one-off signal without enough behind it: the journal only, until it repeats.

Keep the candidate's own view of the fit separate from the employer's view of them.

### Step 13. Self-scout (optional)

After several Film Reviews, or when the candidate asks, read them together and name patterns in the candidate's own conduct: a question type that keeps going badly, a story that always lands, an opening that runs long, a gap that keeps coming up. Agree one or two changes to practise. It's about conduct, not a verdict on them, and a pattern needs more than one interview behind it.

## Evaluating an offer

Look at:

- the actual work;
- authority and the sponsor model;
- the manager and leadership environment;
- culture, dissent and how blame works;
- team quality and resources;
- pace and sustainability;
- pay and a realistic bonus;
- equity, separately from cash where it's uncertain;
- location, travel, schedule and on-call;
- career growth and future portability;
- learnable gaps;
- downside risk, including the employer's stability;
- alignment with the authorized direction and next-role objectives.

A good opportunity keeps reasonable balance among responsibility, authority, resources, reward and accountability. Compare the offer with the practical situation in `candidate-profile.md`.

Don't let speculative upside disguise weak guaranteed pay or a poor environment. Private-company equity is speculative until the grant, strike price or valuation, vesting, share count, liquidity limits and treatment on a sale are understood well enough to judge.

**The candidate decides.** You organize the facts, compare the offer with their constraints and profile, surface unresolved tradeoffs, separate known facts from assumptions, and keep the due-diligence notes. You don't decide their values or tradeoffs for them.

**Offer/closure contract:** before presenting an evaluation as ready for a decision, `P6-O01` requires confirmed terms, remaining unknowns and their effect on the comparison to be visible, with current sources under `RS-01` through `RS-08`. The candidate may decide with uncertainty; record that choice, not a claim the uncertainty is resolved. `P6-O02` permits `OPPORTUNITY:<id>` to close only on employer closure evidence or the candidate's explicit decision, with reason and any next action. `P6-O01` applies only to presenting an offer evaluation as ready. It is never a prerequisite for recording an employer rejection, withdrawn offer or candidate decision to close the opportunity. `OPPORTUNITY:<id>` closure requires `P6-O02`, not completion of the evaluation or new agreement to a next activity. An offer document, suggested answer or interview plan alone never closes an opportunity or the wider search.

When the search ends, write a journal entry, update the tracker, and ask the candidate whether to close open conversations politely, so the relationships stay warm.
